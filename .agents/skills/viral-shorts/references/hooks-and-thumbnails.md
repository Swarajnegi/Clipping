## Verbal hook devices and when to use them

The recognised opening combines three devices: a forceful stop/warning statement,
an **until** condition, and curiosity about **one** unidentified problem. These are
parts of one hook. We may use a comparable device only when the genuine speaker's
words support it and the selected passage supplies the explanation/payoff.

For this channel, choose among these adaptations; the latter three are editorial
applications, not additional formats demonstrated by this file:

- **Warning → correction:** a source genuinely warns against an action and explains
  what to do instead. Reject a warning that ends before the fix.
- **Contrast/reversal → example:** e.g. the same goal, different outcomes. Let a
  genuine contrast lead; use scale/type hierarchy or an original comparison graphic
  to make it legible. Keep the example and conclusion.
- **Personal question → decision frame:** a source asks a relatable question and
  explains a useful way to think about it. Prefer a quiet push and restrained text
  over noisy notification graphics for reflective passages.
- **Unexpected personal admission → earned insight:** let the speaker's account
  supply the surprise; preserve its circumstances and resolution. Do not turn an
  individual experience into a fabricated universal rule.

Do not write a provocative sentence and present it as the speaker's caption.
Editorial headline text must be distinguishable from spoken-word captions and
faithful to the passage. A thumbnail/title cannot rescue weak opening speech.

## Applying a treatment

Plan the first visual transition on the earliest useful spoken beat within
the first three seconds. Let an illustrative cutaway arrive as soon as its
cue makes sense. When the speaker's expression or delivery is the hook, use
a restrained push, shot change or typographic reveal instead. Evaluate the
opening at normal playback speed and keep the speech clear.

Record an opening plan alongside the cut: exact spoken opening, first-three-second
beats, chosen technique and purpose, source/caption timing, graphic provenance,
audio decision and the complete payoff later in the clip. Use only the treatments
that earn their place; applying every stage to every clip is not the objective.

Existing capabilities cover graded footage, shot-aware crops, `punchIns`, timed
editorial caption layers and frame-driven motion. Use an authorised original or
programmatic cutaway/graphic when illustration is useful. More involved illustrated
cards/comparisons can use `motion-edit` after selection. Do not invent renderer
properties or claim an effect was rendered merely because it was described here.

For a novel treatment or uncertain fit, an inexpensive opening preview on one
specific clip is welcome before final exports. Save it as a labelled variant in
`Working`, keeping the clean full proof and audio intact. Show enough of the opening
to assess the treatment, then review the complete passage for the promised payoff.
This is optional; do not add a mandatory approval loop to every routine edit. Respect
an explicit rough-cut approval gate, including the current five pending proposals.

Inspect native/phone-size frames across the first three seconds and transitions;
check faces, gesture clearance, stable phrase layout, actual word onsets and readable
Difference response. Hear the preview when possible and record who reviewed what;
ASR or stills alone do not verify the sound mix. All media work retains the guard.

## Caption placement

Centre the **whole composition**, not every individual word independently. In the
current square-on-black layout, the shared picture/canvas centre is logical (540,960).
Place a phrase elsewhere in genuinely empty picture space when it improves appeal
and readability. Check the actual moving shot rather than assuming one side is empty.
Keep faces and important gestures clear. Change position deliberately at phrase/shot
boundaries; do not make already visible words jump as a phrase builds. Preserve
complete wording, individual word timing, approved fonts, selective true italics and
all-word white Difference. Existing approved projects are not rebuilt automatically.

## Required thumbnail for every finished clip

- Select a sharp, expressive frame from the clean highest-quality source or approved
  master. Keep the source frame/time and any asset provenance. Do not use a blurred,
  compressed social-reference screenshot or copy another channel's cover.
- Create a composed cover with a concise, honest headline tied to the clip's complete
  point. A few strong words usually work better than a transcript paragraph. Keep
  identity, face/eyes, hierarchy and contrast clear at phone size; avoid unsupported
  promises, invented quotes, clutter or foreign overlaid branding.
- Match our approved typography and framing direction. Use cleared bundled fonts;
  an occasional italic accent may support hierarchy. The thumbnail must remain
  readable over bright/dark clothing and at small display sizes.
- For the default vertical 4K delivery, author a 2160×3840 cover and check that its
  important subject/text also survives a centred square crop. Match a deliberately
  horizontal deliverable's cover to that format. Do not call an upscale recovered
  camera detail. Do not insert the static cover into the spoken opening automatically.
- Keep drafts in `Working`; deliver the reviewed `<clip-id>-thumbnail.jpg` or `.png`
  beside **each** finished video in `Exports/<project-id>`. Include its frame/time,
  headline and QA coverage in delivery notes. A final video without its thumbnail
  is an incomplete delivery under this owner instruction. This does not require
  finishing covers for rejected rough cuts or retroactively rebuilding old uploads.
